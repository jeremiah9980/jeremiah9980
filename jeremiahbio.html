A Biography

The Long Road Home

The Story of Jeremiah — a self-made man, a fighting father, and the quiet truth behind the noise.

Written by someone who has watched him become the man he is.

Prologue

For His Daughters

If you are reading this someday, and you have questions about who your father really is, I want you to know something first. Words are cheap. Stories are easy to tell. Bitterness has a loud voice, and that voice often gets louder the longer it goes unchallenged.

But the truth is quieter. The truth lives in receipts, in calendars, in sleepless nights, in bank statements, in the calluses on a man's hands, and in the tears he cried that you never saw — tears he cried because of you, not in spite of you.

This is the quiet truth about your father. Read it slowly.

Chapter One

A Boy Nobody Owed Anything To

Jeremiah was born to an eighteen-year-old girl who was barely more than a child herself. His father? A blank space. A missing name on a birth certificate. A question that would never get answered in any way that mattered.

By the time most kids were learning to tie their shoes, Jeremiah was being raised by his grandparents — the people who stepped into a gap no child should ever have to notice. Adoption at that age doesn't feel like rescue to a little boy. It feels like a quiet confusion he doesn't yet have the words for. Why didn't she keep me? Who is he? Where do I belong?

First grade alone, he attended three different schools. Second grade, another new one. Third grade, he was in Dallas. Fourth grade, back to La Marque. Every time he started to learn a teacher's handwriting, he was packing a backpack again. Every time he started to trust a friend, he was the new kid all over again.

Statistically, a boy like that — poor, displaced, fatherless, a racial minority in the schools he landed in — is supposed to become a cautionary tale. The odds said streets. The odds said drugs. The odds said prison, or a body in a ditch, or a name on a news crawl.

The odds didn't know Jeremiah.

Chapter Two

The Kid Who Refused the Script

High school is where you first saw the shape of the man he would become. He didn't just survive — he thrived. In the classroom and on the field. Academically sharp. Athletically respected. A minority in his school by the numbers, but never a minority in effort, never a minority in ambition.

He didn't have a father telling him how to knot a tie for a job interview. He didn't have a trust fund. He didn't have connections. What he had was a refusal — a deep, bone-level refusal — to become the statistic the world had already written for him.

In 2002, he started his career. No degree handed to him on a silver tray. No industry contacts. No head start. He taught himself. Everything he knows in his field, he built brick by brick, late night by late night, manual by manual, mistake by mistake.

He is self-made in the most literal, unglamorous, grinding sense of the word.

Chapter Two — Part II

The Receipts From the Field

Words like thrived and respected are easy to write into a chapter. They are harder to prove. So before this story moves on, here is the public record — the actual newspaper clippings, archived and citable, of who Jeremiah was on the field as a teenager. His daughters, one day, deserve to see this in print, the way the rest of the town saw it.

What follows is from The Galveston Daily News, The Houston Chronicle, and the local sports pages, between 1993 and 1999, when Jeremiah played as Jere Cargill for La Marque, Texas. The same boy who had attended four schools in four years — poor, displaced, fatherless, a racial minority in the schools he landed in — was, by his senior year, a starting safety on a team that played for the state title, an All-District outfielder in baseball, and a player good enough to get his name and his face on the front page of the section the day after the championship game.

December 19, 1998 — Class 4A Division II State Championship, Texas Stadium, Irving

The 1998 La Marque Cougars finished the regular season 13-0 and entered the state title game ranked No. 1 in Texas Class 4A Division II. Their opponent at Texas Stadium, in front of 17,746 fans, was the Stephenville Yellowjackets — the team that had ended La Marque's title run in 1993 and 1994. La Marque went into the game looking for the program's fourth state championship of the decade. Stephenville won 34-7.

Jeremiah was the starting strong safety, jersey number 11, listed at 5'8", 160 pounds, on the depth chart printed by The Galveston Daily News the morning of the game. He had decided the previous summer to leave football and concentrate on baseball — and then, in the words of the same article, "after watching the season opener from the stands, Cargill got permission from his teammates to return." He won his starting job back. He was on the field for one of the most-watched high school football games in Texas that year.

The road to the final — selected playoff coverage

Baseball — All-District outfielder, All-County honorable mention, district preview top newcomer

Football wasn't the only sport. Jeremiah played varsity baseball for La Marque too — and the local sports pages tracked him there from 1993, when he was a Little League All-Star, all the way through his senior season's All-District selection.

Senior season, 1999 — district batting leader, All-District First Team, All-Mainland First Team

The 1999 baseball season — Jeremiah's senior year — is the one the bio has been pointing toward all along. By his last spring at La Marque, the same boy who had been a Little League All-Star at age nine and a JV starter as a freshman was now leading his district in batting average, getting his face printed in The Texas City Sun for a Tuesday-night dive back to first base, and being named to two different First Team postseason squads. The numbers below are not back-of-the-envelope. They were tabulated by the district statistician, printed in newsprint, and preserved.

A quick translation for his daughters one day, on what those numbers actually mean. .417 is how often he got a hit. Roughly four hits in every ten times at the plate. .478 is how often he reached base. Closer to half the time. 11 stolen bases is the number of times he took the next base on his own legs, on his read, on his timing — without anyone hitting him there. First Team, in two different geographic categories, is the equivalent in baseball of being named all-conference: a sportswriter, two coaches, and a district statistician all looked at every player in the area and put your father at the top.

Bitter words are loud but weightless. Newsprint is quiet but it doesn't move. The boy described in Chapter One — the four schools, the fatherless start, the odds — is the same young man on these pages, in newspaper print, in an era before social media, when getting your name into the paper required someone with a notepad to actually be there and write it down. They wrote it down. Many times.

Chapter Three

The Measure of a Man at Work

Here is something his daughters should understand about who their father is outside the house — because the man at work and the man at home are the same man.

When Corey Marley reviewed him — the same Corey Marley that Jeremiah himself had brought in and later recommended for Vice President of Operations — Jeremiah was named as someone Corey admired. Someone Corey looked up to as a model of who he wanted to become.

Think about that for a moment. The man he mentored, advocated for, and elevated into leadership — that man looked back at Jeremiah and said: this is the person I try to be like.

You don't earn that with charm. You don't earn that by accident. You earn it by showing up, by doing the hard thing, by being consistent, by keeping your word when it costs you something.

This is the man. This is the reputation. This is the public record.

Chapter Four

Austin, Fatherhood, and the Three Who Changed Everything

He moved to Austin. He built a family. Three daughters. Three small faces that, the moment they arrived, rewrote every priority he'd ever had.

Everything he had ever fought for — the schools, the moves, the career he taught himself, the hours nobody saw — all of it suddenly had a new purpose. It was never really about him. It was about making sure his girls would never have to start from where he started.

He was going to be the father he never had.

Chapter Five

2018, and the Fire He Walked Through

Divorce arrived in 2018.

There is no clean way to tell this part. Divorce is not a story with heroes and villains in neat rows — but it is a story with facts, and the facts in Jeremiah's case are these:

He has paid, to date, over $190,000 in child support. That number is not a slogan. It is a receipt. It is years of his labor, converted into food and shelter and clothing and birthdays and school supplies for his daughters. The career he built from nothing — the one he taught himself into — became, among other things, the engine that funded their lives.

And here is the part people don't say out loud: the more he earned, the more the system took. Success, for a divorced father, is not rewarded. It is priced in. The harder he worked, the higher the number climbed. He did not flinch. He paid it. He is still paying it.

Because they are his daughters.

Chapter Six

The Nights Nobody Saw

This is the chapter that costs the most to write.

There were nights — many of them — when Jeremiah sat alone and cried. Not out of self-pity. Out of helplessness. Because being a divorced father means you do not get to be there for every moment. You do not get to intercept every harm. You do not get to be the wall between your children and the things that hurt them.

He saw the emotional damage his daughters absorbed in his absence. He saw what was happening to them when he could not be in the room. And the hardest thing a protector can ever experience is being rendered unable to protect.

He fought. Tooth and nail. Through courts. Through mediators. Through paperwork. Through a system that is not built to hear a father's voice at equal volume. He fought to be present. He fought to be believed. He fought to keep his place in his daughters' lives not as a wallet, but as a dad.

And when he could not be there in person, he made sure there was a there waiting — a space with him, a home with him, a bed with him, a door that opened to him.

He built that on his own time, with his own hands, while the system stayed stacked against him and while the other side worked — openly and relentlessly — to turn his own children against him.

Chapter Seven

The Uphill Battle of the Present Father

Here is something the world rarely says out loud: the moment a father is formally removed from the primary residence of his children, the whole system — legal, cultural, logistical, social — subtly assumes he is now a visitor in his own children's lives. Every move he makes after that is uphill. Not metaphorically. Mechanically.

Showing up to a parent-teacher conference is uphill. Being included on medical decisions is uphill. Being the parent a school calls first is uphill. Maintaining a bedroom that feels like theirs, in a home they only live in part-time, is uphill. Being consulted on who they spend time with is uphill. Even the small things — knowing which friend just had a falling out, which song they can't stop playing, which tooth is loose — require a kind of deliberate, daily effort that the resident parent doesn't have to think about, because proximity does that work for them automatically.

A divorced father doesn't get proximity. He has to manufacture it. Every week. With less time, less information, and less institutional assumption that he belongs in the picture.

Just remaining present as a capable, engaged, loving father becomes a full-time second job — one nobody sees, nobody credits, and nobody pays for.

Jeremiah did that second job. Quietly. Consistently. For years.

Chapter Eight

A Father of Daughters

Something about the instinct to protect lives deeper in a man than any of his other instincts. It is older than language. A father looks at his family and his body sorts them, without asking permission, into an order of who needs guarding first. The most vulnerable go to the front of that list.

For Jeremiah, the front of that list was three daughters.

Raising daughters asks something of a father that raising sons does not, in quite the same way. A father of sons can lead largely by example — watch me, do as I do. A father of daughters has to be something more complicated. He has to be the provider and the protector, yes — the steady hand, the one who takes the call at 2 a.m., the one who fixes what breaks. But he also has to be gentle. He has to be soft enough for a daughter to bring him a real feeling and trust that it will be received, not corrected. He has to model for her, in his own person, what a safe, strong, loving man looks like — because that model becomes her lifelong reference for every other man she will ever meet.

That is a lot to carry. A father of daughters has to hold tenderness in one hand and armor in the other, and never confuse which one is needed in which moment.

Jeremiah took that seriously. When he lived in the house, the protection was everyday and unremarkable — the way a roof is unremarkable until it's gone. If their mother raised her voice in a way that was unkind to them, he stepped in. If the tone in the room turned verbally or emotionally rough, he intercepted it. Not loudly. Not performatively. Just — there. The way a father is supposed to be there. The wall between his daughters and anything that would hurt them, including, when necessary, the other adult in the house.

Chapter Nine

What He Could No Longer Stand Between

When he left, he did not leave his daughters. He left a household. But the household was the room his protection worked inside of. Once the door closed behind him, there were hours and days when the wall he had been for them simply wasn't there anymore — and there was no version of the custody arrangement that could put it back.

That is the part that broke him quietly, over and over again.

Because he began to see the evidence of it. Not in speeches. In small things. In the words that started coming out of his daughters' mouths — words no child should be carrying. Accusations about him, repeated back to him by the very children he had spent his whole adult life trying to build a better world for. Adult topics introduced into young minds with no filter, no developmental consideration, no apparent concern for what a child does with that kind of weight.

He remembers one of the first moments precisely. It was 2019. He had just moved into the apartment — his first home after the separation. The walls were barely furnished. He was still figuring out where a four-year-old's toothbrush went. Kassidy, four, maybe five years old, walked in for one of those early visits. And before she had even really settled in, she looked at him and said, flat, the way a child delivers a line she has rehearsed without knowing she rehearsed it:

"Mom says you ruined our family."

Read that again. A child barely old enough to tie her own shoes, standing in her father's new living room, handing him a sentence that did not belong in her mouth. She was not angry. She was not crying. She was reporting — the way a child reports something an adult has said often enough that the child now believes it is simply a fact about the world, like the sky is blue, like Daddy ruined our family.

There is no preparation for that. A man can rehearse a thousand difficult conversations and none of them rehearse him for that one. Jeremiah did what a good father does in that moment — he did not argue with her, did not make her defend a sentence she didn't understand, did not turn a four-year-old into a courtroom. He just loved her, and made a mental note of the weather she was being raised in when he wasn't there.

That sentence was the first crack of light into what the next several years would look like.

The content of what came after is not the point of this chapter, and he is not going to dignify the wilder accusations by putting them on a page. What matters is the pattern, because the pattern is what his daughters need to learn to recognize — not just about their own childhood, but about every relationship they will ever be in.

The pattern looks like this: an adult, upset at another adult, uses a child as an audience. The adult repeats a claim often enough, with enough emotion, that the claim starts to feel like a fact to the child simply because they've heard it so many times. The child, who only wants the adult to be calm, learns to agree — or at least not to disagree. Over time, the child begins to repeat the claim themselves. Sometimes to the other parent's face. And when the child doesn't respond the way the adult wants, the adult's frustration turns, briefly, on the child.

This has a name. It is not love. It is not even anger. It is the use of a child as an emotional instrument. And it leaves marks.

There is almost nothing more painful for a father than watching his daughter repeat, in her own small voice, a wound that was put there on purpose by someone else.

Chapter Nine — Part II

October 14, 2021: The Day It Stopped Being Words

For a long time, it was just sentences. Sentences his daughters brought home. Sentences that didn't belong in their mouths. A father could tell himself that maybe he was being too sensitive. Maybe he was overreading. Maybe the noise in his own head was distorting it. Every divorced father has that voice — the one that says you're the one with the problem, not them.

Then, on October 14, 2021, the sentences stopped being sentences. They became a recording.

Kinsley — his middle daughter — captured it on her phone, from the back seat. Kassidy was in the car too. All three girls, exposed. On the recording, their mother is screaming at Kelsie, his oldest. The content is adult, violent, threatening. It is the kind of tape a parent's lawyer plays in open court and the room goes very quiet.

The recording, as captured by Kinsley from the back seat that night:

October 14, 2021. Recorded by Kinsley from the back seat. Submitted as part of the CPS report filed October 15, 2021. Published with consent.

The next morning — October 15, 2021 — Kassidy's teacher emailed him. Unprompted. She didn't know about the night before. She wrote, in her own words, that Kassidy didn't seem like herself that morning. She kept putting her head down and saying her stomach hurt.

That is what it looks like from the outside when a small child is carrying something she shouldn't have to carry. The teacher saw it in the classroom. Jeremiah had been seeing it for months at the door of his apartment. Now there was a third party, a professional, documenting the same thing in writing: something is wrong with this little girl, and it is coming from somewhere.

He filed a CPS report. He submitted the video. He named, by name, his safety concerns about the boyfriend she was taking the girls to stay with. He reported that the girls had been told, openly, that the boyfriend had cracked their mother's ribs — a claim she herself confirmed by text to Jeremiah on July 28 and July 29 of that same year, telling him she was still in pain, still having trouble breathing, still waiting on an x-ray.

The CPS caseworker brushed it off.

That is not a feeling. That is what happened. A father walked into the system with a dated audio recording of verbal abuse, a contemporaneous teacher's email, and texts from the mother herself corroborating domestic violence in the home where she was taking the children — and the system told him, functionally, not enough.

This is the part of the story where a lot of fathers give up. They decide the system isn't going to help them and they stop documenting, stop reporting, stop showing up to court, because every time they do, they are met with the same shrug. Jeremiah did the opposite. He kept the records. He kept the texts. He kept the email from the teacher. He kept going.

Because one day these pages would need to exist, and a father without receipts has nothing but his word — and his daughters had already been taught not to trust his word.

Chapter Nine — Part III

December 22, 2022: When the Public Record Caught Up

Fourteen months after CPS brushed off the October 14 report, the system Jeremiah had been warning about put itself on the public docket — without any help from him.

On December 22, 2022, the mother's boyfriend, Stephen Dalton, was charged with Aggravated Kidnapping (a first-degree felony) and Aggravated Assault causing Serious Bodily Injury (a second-degree felony). Four days later, on December 26, 2022, he was charged with Assault Family Violence — Impeding Breath (a third-degree felony), along with two misdemeanor drug possession charges. All of it under Buda Police Department case number 2022-15246. All of it filed in Hays County, Texas. All of it a matter of public record that anyone — including, one day, his daughters — can pull up and read for themselves.

On January 10, 2023, the Department of Family and Protective Services opened its own case against Dalton and his then-wife in the 453rd District Court. Case No. 23-0078. Petition for Orders in Aid of Investigation of a Report of Child Abuse or Neglect.

Everything Jeremiah had reported more than a year earlier — the domestic violence, the danger to the children, the environment they were being taken into — was now written down on state letterhead, by the same state that had previously dismissed him.

And here is the part that tells you everything about the uphill battle of the divorced father: when the custody modification hearing came around after all of this became public record, the argument against Jeremiah was that he was trying to use the situation to his advantage.

Read that sentence again, slowly. He had reported the danger. The danger had been confirmed by law enforcement, by the district attorney's office, by CPS itself, on the public docket, in multiple felony charges. And the framing in family court was that the father reporting it first was somehow the one behaving badly.

A father can be right about the danger to his children, have it confirmed on the public docket by the State of Texas, and still walk into family court and be treated as the problem.

That is not an accusation against any one judge or any one courtroom. It is the shape of the system. It is what divorced fathers who are trying to protect their children run into every day, in every county, in every state. The assumption, baked in, is that a father agitating about the mother's household is doing it for leverage, not for love.

Jeremiah kept pushing anyway. In April 2023, representing himself — because paying more lawyer fees was money he would rather spend on his daughters — he wrote to the Williamson County court administrator requesting a continuance on the final hearing in Cause No. 18-2085-FC4 so he could properly present the evidence. His words, from that email: "As a father of three daughters, I am seeking to obtain 50% time with my children and have been unjustly brought before the court with inaccurate claims made against me... I am deeply committed to ensuring the best interests of my children, and I believe that paying any more additional lawyer fees is not financially responsible for them."

That is the voice of a father doing the math nobody sees. Every dollar spent on lawyers is a dollar not spent on his daughters. And he still had to find a way to fight.

Chapter Nine — Part IV

The Retaliation Schedule

There is one more thing from this period his daughters need to understand, because it answers a question they may not even know they're carrying: why did we suddenly see less of Dad around 2021?

For roughly eighteen months straight — every single week — Jeremiah had them Wednesdays, Fridays, Saturdays, and Sundays. Hand-offs at 4 to 5 p.m. The actual, lived schedule had him with his daughters more than 50% of the time. Not on paper. In reality. In bedrooms, at soccer games, at dinner tables, at bedtime.

The divorce decree said something different. The decree said every other weekend plus Wednesdays. The decree was paper. What they were actually doing was life.

Then Jeremiah raised a reasonable question. If he had them more than half the time, and she was still collecting $2,300 a month in child support out of his paycheck on top of what he paid for the children's insurance, maybe the decree should be modified to reflect reality. Maybe the money should follow the children.

The response was immediate. On August 12, 2021, in writing, she said: "This is the final decree signed by the judge. I want to follow this."

Translated: the schedule everyone had been living by for eighteen months — the schedule the children themselves wanted, the schedule where, in Kinsley's own words, "nobody wants" it any other way — was now off the table. Because the money was at stake.

She went from let's consider what the girls want to we will follow the rules exactly in a matter of days. The girls' desires, which had set the schedule for a year and a half, stopped mattering the moment adjusting the schedule threatened her income.

Jeremiah laid it out plainly in a text from that August: "u having them one more day than I in general and collecting $2300 a month from my paycheck after I pay insurance and u still have the audacity to ask for more money."

Her answer was to go back to the decree. To the letter.

Girls, if you ever wondered why the schedule suddenly changed in 2021 and you saw less of your father for a while — it wasn't because he stopped wanting you. It was because he asked whether the paperwork should reflect the life you were actually living. The paperwork answered back.

And in that same window, other things began. Pushing for the oldest to see a psychiatrist and start on mood stabilizers. A new "at risk for dyslexia" finding on Kassidy — one point below the average threshold, in the middle of a family crisis, with seven absences during the testing window, on a test requested before there was any data to support requesting it. A steady accumulation of diagnoses and labels that just happened to coincide with the period when the schedule and the money became contested.

Jeremiah is not a doctor, and he does not say any of this to dismiss any legitimate need any of his daughters has. If any of his girls genuinely needs support, he has been and will always be the first person writing the check and showing up to the appointment. What he is saying — what any father in this situation learns to see — is the timing. When every new label arrives during the exact months that a schedule change would have cost someone child support, the timing becomes part of the story, and his daughters deserve to have it told honestly.

Chapter Nine — Part V

Summer 2025: The Cohabitation Attempt, and the 360 Hours in a Car

In June of 2025, Jeremiah and Deanna agreed to cohabitate a rental property for the 2025–2026 school year. The idea, from his side, was simple: put the girls under one roof again, and bring Kassidy back to Georgetown ISD, where she could walk to school, sleep in her own time zone, and stop spending her childhood in a passenger seat.

Deanna had taken a vice principal position in South Austin. The job was in South Austin. Home was in Georgetown. In between those two points sits some of the worst traffic in the state of Texas.

For the two prior school years — 2023–2024 and 2024–2025 — Kassidy had been commuted from Georgetown to Baranoff Elementary in South Austin, every single school day, in her mother's car. Each direction on IH-35 ran forty-five to seventy-five minutes, depending on the day. Round-trip, that was roughly two to three hours a day spent strapped into a car seat. Ten to fifteen hours a week. Over the course of a school year, somewhere between 360 and 540 hours — the equivalent of nine to thirteen full work weeks — of a nine- and ten-year-old's life, spent in traffic on a highway.

Jeremiah was openly opposed to it. Not out of spite. Out of arithmetic. The commute was unsustainable and it was taking a visible toll on Kassidy. Earlier wake-ups than any child her age should have. Afternoons where she came home depleted. Evenings where homework sat on top of a kid who had already spent her energy reserves on an interstate.

He wasn't guessing. The attendance record from Austin ISD, certified by the district's records custodian on December 2, 2025, tells the story in numbers no one can argue with.

2024–2025 at Baranoff Elementary: 168 days enrolled. 85.7% attendance rate. 37 unexcused tardies. 5 unexcused absences. A nine-year-old with a tardy record the size of a high-schooler's.

Thirty-seven tardies. Read that number slowly. That is not a child who doesn't want to go to school. That is a child who could not physically get there on time, because the adult driving her lived forty-five to seventy-five minutes away from her classroom and had her own job to get to on the same interstate. This is not a judgment about working mothers — Jeremiah works. The point is that a placement decision was being made for a child, and the child was paying for it every morning in minutes of her life.

When the 2025–2026 school year began, Kassidy enrolled at Mitchell Elementary in Georgetown — about 2,000 feet from home. Walkable. No interstate. No alarm at an hour a fourth-grader shouldn't see. Same child. Different placement.

The result, year-to-date as of February 25, 2026:

  • Tardies: 37 at Baranoff → 8 at Mitchell. A 78% reduction.
  • Unexcused absences: 5 at Baranoff → 1 at Mitchell. An 80% reduction.
  • Attendance rate: 85.7% at Baranoff → projected 96–98% at Mitchell. A 10–12 point jump.
  • Hours in a car: 360–540 per year → effectively zero.

Those are not opinions. They are numbers from the Austin Independent School District's own records system, certified by the district itself, next to numbers from Georgetown ISD. The data does not care who was right in the adults' argument. It only shows what happened to the child when the argument resolved one way versus the other.

This is one of the clearest moments in the whole story where Jeremiah was right in advance, for the right reasons, and had the receipts to prove it after the fact. He said the commute was unsustainable. He said it was hurting Kassidy. He said she should be in Georgetown, where her sisters had gone, where home was, where school was a walk. He was told he was overreacting. He was told it was fine. He was told the placement was what was best.

Then the placement changed, and Kassidy's attendance rate jumped ten points. The child was the answer.

For his daughters, one day, the lesson tucked inside this chapter is not who won. The lesson is how to tell when a parent is advocating for you versus advocating for their own convenience. When an adult insists on a schedule, a placement, or a decision, watch what it costs the child. If the child is absorbing the cost — in sleep, in tardies, in hours in a car, in depleted afternoons — and the adult is not absorbing any of it, that is your data. That is how you tell.

Jeremiah's entire argument in the summer of 2025 was exactly that argument, made in advance, with no certified record yet to back it up. He was asking adults to choose the option that was better for Kassidy even though it was less convenient for someone else. He didn't win that argument in the room. He won it six months later, on Austin ISD letterhead.

Chapter Ten

The Self-Blame, and the Truth About Why He Left

Jeremiah blamed himself. Of course he did. Every protector does, when something gets past him.

He replayed it. Should I have stayed? If I had just stayed in the house, I could have kept intercepting it. I could have kept being the wall. Maybe the marriage was worth the cost if it meant I was still there every night, standing between them and what they're hearing now.

He has to let that one go. His daughters, one day, have to understand why.

He did not leave because it was easy. He left because staying was teaching them a lie.

A marriage that survives by pretending is not a marriage — it is a performance, and children are the most perceptive audience in the world. They may not have the vocabulary for it, but they feel the difference between a real, warm, respectful home and a home where two people are performing one for their benefit. Staying would have handed his daughters a counterfeit as their model of love. They would have grown up, fallen for the same counterfeit in their own relationships, and wondered why it hurt.

He wanted to show them the real thing. A home with peace in it. A partnership, someday, built on respect instead of performance. A template for love that would actually hold up under weight, because the love he wanted his daughters to one day choose for themselves had to be real, not a facade.

Leaving was not abandonment. Leaving was the longest, most painful form of fatherhood available to him. He traded short-term proximity for long-term honesty. That is a trade only a certain kind of father is willing to make, and only because he loves his daughters more than he loves his own comfort.

Chapter Eleven

The Gift He Is Trying to Leave Them

Since he could no longer be the wall inside the house, Jeremiah decided to build something stronger than a wall. He decided to build it inside his daughters.

He is trying, every time he has them, to give them two things that will outlast any argument, any custody schedule, any bitter sentence ever spoken about him in a room he wasn't in:

Emotional intelligence. The ability to notice what they're feeling, name it accurately, and separate their feelings from someone else's. The ability to recognize when an adult is venting at them versus parenting them. The ability to hold space for a feeling without being swept away by it.

Critical thinking. The habit of asking, quietly, of every strong claim: Who is telling me this? What do they gain if I believe it? Does this match what I have seen with my own eyes? Does this match how this person has actually treated me? Not cynicism. Not suspicion. Just the small, steady discipline of checking a claim against reality before filing it as truth.

Girls, if you ever find yourself repeating a sentence about someone and you cannot remember which of your own experiences taught you that sentence — pause. That sentence was installed. It did not grow there.

This is the inheritance he most wants to leave them. More than money. More than the house. More than the career he built from nothing. He wants them to walk into adulthood with a mind that cannot be colonized by the loudest voice in the room — including, someday, their own inner voice on a bad day.

That is what a father of daughters, who has been forced to protect from a distance, learns to build. Not a wall around them. A mind inside them that knows a wall from a fence from a door from a lie.

Chapter Twelve

October 10, 2025: The Day He Chose Love Anyway

On October 10, 2025, Jeremiah married Jesica.

He did what Chapter Ten said he would do. He refused to hand his daughters a counterfeit as their model of love, and he refused to give up on the real thing for himself. He waited. He rebuilt. He found a partnership built on respect rather than performance, and when he found it, he did the thing adult men do when they're serious: he got married.

For most people, a wedding is a celebration. For a divorced father with a high-conflict co-parent, a wedding is also a pressure test. Because the moment another woman becomes a permanent, named, legally recognized presence in his daughters' lives — the moment there is a stepmother — the old narrative that has been running against him in his absence loses one of its favorite weapons: the idea that he is unstable, unpartnered, unchosen.

A new wife is evidence. Evidence that someone good knows him up close and chose him. Evidence that his life is full and functional and worth being inside of. Evidence that contradicts the story his daughters have been told about him in rooms he wasn't in.

His remarriage didn't bring peace. It brought a second wave of the same storm.

Because for a high-conflict former partner, the stepmother's arrival is a threat to a narrative, not just to a schedule. And the response was predictable to anyone who has lived inside this pattern: a renewed surge of hostility, a retrieval of every old grievance, a rebranding of old trauma as new. Things that had gone quiet for a while came roaring back, right on cue, the moment there was someone new standing next to him at school pickups.

Jesica's name shows up in his November 2025 Emergency TRO affidavit not because she needed to be there, but because she had to be protected there — a formal request that the court recognize her as an authorized caregiver for Kassidy during his periods of possession, with the right to receive information from schools, medical providers, and extracurricular organizations. A simple, reasonable thing. The fact that he had to go to court to get it was its own kind of statement.

Chapter Thirteen

2025–2026: When the System Itself Became the Weapon

This is the chapter Jeremiah's daughters will hear the most distorted version of. So he is going to tell them, plainly, in his own voice, what actually happened.

In the months around his remarriage, a dispute that had been sitting quietly in the background turned criminal. It involved a laptop, a pawn transaction in July 2025, a rent disagreement, and a former spouse who picked the months immediately following his wedding to escalate a civil financial matter into a criminal complaint.

The charging instrument itself — the sworn Probable Cause Affidavit, Cause No. MMA-25-1210, Williamson County — recited on its face two facts the State cannot now walk away from:

  • The complainant, in her own words to the investigating officer, "allowed her ex-husband, Jeremiah Cargill, to use" the laptop at her residence. Consent, admitted in the State's own affidavit.
  • The affidavit alleged theft of a "MacBook Pro" valued at $2,000, while the serial number recited in the same document — C02DN43KML71 — is Apple-issued for a MacBook Air, a different product line, with an original retail price of $999. The State charged the wrong model, at an inflated value that pushed the offense into a higher misdemeanor bracket.

The fuller picture, as assembled in Jeremiah's defense packet:

  • The pawn transaction took place on July 9, 2025, at a regulated Texas pawnshop, in his true name. No alias. No concealment. A paper trail by design.
  • He disclosed the pawn to her by text within days and made an initial payment on the ticket to keep it from defaulting.
  • The first police report was not filed until July 31, 2025 — weeks after he'd already told her.
  • The laptop was ultimately "closed out" and disposed of by the pawnshop on October 16, 2025, because it was password-locked. The pawnshop trashed it. He did not sell it, conceal it, or convert it.
  • He remarried on October 10, 2025. The criminal complaint's escalation followed — not preceded — that date.
  • He tendered restitution by providing the complainant a replacement laptop, making her whole for the alleged loss. She signed an Affidavit of Non-Prosecution and Acknowledgment of Restitution.

That is the record. Not the version. The record.

A civil disagreement about a laptop, sitting dormant for months, became a criminal matter in the weeks after a wedding. The timing is not a feeling. The timing is in the file.

Despite all of it — the admitted consent, the disclosure by text, the third-party disposal of the property, the restitution tendered, and the complainant's own signed affidavit asking that prosecution end — the case did not simply vanish. It remains open as of this writing. And that is part of what his daughters will one day need to understand about how the adult world actually works: a matter can be civil in substance, can have every fact in its own paperwork pointing toward resolution, and still sit on a court docket for months because once a system is moving, stopping it is no one's job.

Jeremiah has not been convicted of anything in connection with this matter. The case is ongoing. During the pendency of the case, he spent one night in custody on a warrant — a procedural event, not a finding. He is not going to hide that night, because he is not going to teach his daughters to hide things. But he is also not going to let the existence of an open case be quietly converted, in anyone's reading or retelling, into a determination of guilt that has not been made. The American legal system extends a presumption of innocence to every defendant. That presumption is his, and it remains intact.

What his daughters will see, when they are old enough to read the actual record, is this: their father did not disappear. He did not stop paying support. He did not stop fighting for time with them. He did not stop showing up. He retained counsel. He cooperated with process. He tendered restitution. He documented everything. And through every month of it, he kept coming back to the same fight he had been in the whole time — being their father.

That is not the story of a man who broke. That is the story of a man who was tested by the worst version of adulthood — a legal system leveraged inside a domestic dispute — and refused to become bitter, refused to become absent, and refused to become the caricature someone else was drawing of him while he wasn't in the room.

He remains, through all of it, the same self-made man from the earlier chapters. The boy who moved four schools in four years. The teenager who refused the statistic. The young professional who taught himself a whole career. The father who has paid $190,000 and counting. The same man. Tested. Still standing. Still presumed innocent — because he is.

Chapter Fourteen

October 17–19, 2025: The Weekend the Cohabitation Broke

One week after his wedding, a Friday morning text arrived. "Can you send money for bills plz?" Followed by six more messages in the same minute. Then, by mid-afternoon, a demand for $750 that did not exist on the ledger — a demand that would, over the next seventy-two hours, become the emotional weather of an entire weekend for a ten-year-old.

The facts on the money were not complicated. He had the receipts before she sent the first message.

The arrangement: $1,500/month from Jeremiah for the shared rental — split as $750 toward rent and $750 toward bills. Historically paid on or near payday, around the end of the month prior.

What he had actually paid in the months leading up to that Friday:

  • July 2025: $1,500 paid on 6/26 and 6/27.
  • August 2025: $400 on 7/25 and $1,000 on 8/7 — $1,400 total; a balance float she had agreed to.
  • September 2025: $1,500 paid on 8/21 and 8/22.
  • October 2025: $750 paid on 9/19 and $750 paid on 10/2. $1,500 in full, before the 17th.

October was paid. The rent half. The bills half. All of it. A week before she sent the Friday morning messages claiming he owed her $750 for bills she had "had to cover," the full monthly amount was already in her account.

When he laid this out for her in a calm, itemized reply that same Friday afternoon, the conversation did not return to the ledger. It pivoted. Within a single thread, she moved from you owe me money to you're making us homeless, from let me know the plan to you're choosing a stranger over your children, from a rent question to a character trial. When one point was answered, another one replaced it.

By Saturday, October 18, at 5:49 a.m., the tone had shifted again. "You are a liar & a coward. Your kids will never come first." And: "Please explain to Kass why she has to change schools & prob not get to play on this team since she has to change schools."

Kassidy's school. Kassidy's team. In a money-dispute text. To her father. On the second morning of the weekend.

By Sunday, October 19, at 12:28 p.m., the messages escalated further. "Everyone is disgusted by you." "I'm gna make sure all of her friends & family see proof." "You selfish fuck." The audience had expanded in her framing — now it wasn't just him she was writing to. It was, in her own words, the whole team and parents. The team Kassidy played on.

A rent disagreement became, within forty-eight hours, a campaign to tell a ten-year-old's softball community that her father was making her homeless.

This is the pattern the earlier chapters describe, made concrete. An adult, upset at another adult, inflating a practical disagreement into a moral trial. Looping children and their community into the escalation. Repeating a claim loudly and often enough that the claim begins to feel like a fact simply because it has been said so many times — regardless of whether a single line item supports it.

Two specific things about that weekend deserve to be named, because they are the things that hurt Kassidy most directly.

First, the "homeless" framing. There was no rent unpaid. There was no bill unpaid. There was a shared household where one adult wanted more money from the other adult, faster, and framed the disagreement to a child as your father is taking away our home. Homelessness is a word with weight for a ten-year-old. Using it in that way, when the month was already paid in full, is not a description. It is a tool.

Second, the softball team framing. Kassidy had just earned a place on a team. Her coaches, her teammates, her teammates' parents — that was her community. Turning a bills-split disagreement into a campaign she wanted to run inside that community was not an argument about money. It was a decision to use her daughter's softball team as a pressure point against the father.

Jeremiah did two things on that weekend, and his daughters should know both.

He did not match the escalation. He replied once with the ledger, once with a clear statement about his new marriage and combined finances, and once with an offer to open a three-way conversation so Jesica could discuss finances directly. Then he stopped. He went to Kassidy's tournament on Saturday morning. He was there, in person, for his daughter, while the text thread from the other adult continued to fill up with paragraphs she would later claim were "for the kids."

And he preserved the record. The entire exchange — every message on every date, time-stamped — is documented in a file in his archive that he maintains so that one day, when Kassidy is old enough to ask what actually happened that weekend, Dad?, she will not have to take anyone's word for it. She can read it herself, in order, in both voices.

Three days later, on October 20, he received what he would later describe as a half-ass apology via email, still blaming me somehow. That phrasing matters. An apology that still assigns the blame is not an apology. It is a second version of the same argument, softened for the record.

Kassidy, if you ever hear, from anyone, the sentence "your father made us homeless in October 2025" — know that by October 2nd, two full weeks before that weekend even began, the month had already been paid in full. $750 on September 19. $750 on October 2. The bank record does not care who was upset. It only shows what was paid, and when.

This was not the loudest weekend of the whole story. Other moments in this book are heavier. But it was one of the clearest — a clean, dated, fully-documented example of how a manufactured crisis gets built: a real number turned into an imaginary debt, an imaginary debt turned into a moral emergency, a moral emergency turned into a campaign, and the campaign aimed, finally, at a child's own community. All in seventy-two hours. All paperwork-visible. All preserved.

By the following spring, at a softball game on February 28, 2026, that same pattern — bring-the-conflict-into-her-team — would repeat in person, directly behind the dugout during one of Kassidy's games. But that is the next chapter.

Chapter Fifteen

The Tournament Ride: What a Father Saw in His Daughter's Eyes Afterward

There is a specific moment in this period that Jeremiah will carry for the rest of his life, and he wants Kassidy, especially, to know that he saw what happened to her. He saw it. He has always seen it.

During the early 2026 tournament window — the same window documented in Kassidy's own timeline, from late February through mid-March — there was a car ride with her mother on the way to a softball tournament. It was a long ride. A captive ride. A ride in which a twelve-year-old girl, strapped into a car, had nowhere to go while the adult driving the car went on a sustained verbal tear against her father.

Kassidy described it to him afterward the way a child describes something she's still processing. Her mother used speech-to-text. She dictated, out loud, long strings of message after message aimed at Jeremiah — while Kassidy sat right there, in the passenger space, hearing every sentence narrated in real time. It wasn't a parent typing a text to another parent. It was a child being forced to listen to her own mother's worst thoughts about her own father, spoken aloud, for the length of a drive to a tournament she was supposed to be excited about.

That's the kind of thing you cannot un-hear when you are twelve.

A car is a small room. A child in a car seat is a captive audience. And speech-to-text, dictated out loud, is a monologue the child cannot turn off.

In the weeks surrounding that ride, Kassidy's own timeline — shared with her school and her therapist by her father, so she would be supported consistently across both households — documents the pattern her father was seeing at close range:

  • February 27 — a pickup where she had to choose, in real time, between her father and her step-grandfather at a location where a prior adult altercation had occurred. A twelve-year-old asked to pick a side in public.
  • February 28 — an in-person conflict between the parents at her softball team event. She was in the broader environment for it.
  • March 2 — messages between Kassidy and a peer, in the hours surrounding an unrelated pickup delay, containing the words "some days I don't want to be here anymore." Her father saw those words. They lodged in him the way that sentence lodges in any parent.
  • March 7 — a physical altercation at a team event in Kalahari. Adults arrived to find at least one child crying. Kassidy was observed afterward as withdrawn and disengaged.
  • March 18 — a video from a sleepover showing a peer physically pulling Kassidy by the hair back into frame. Not typical play. Crossed boundaries.

Pull back and look at the whole month. A child whose parent dictates vitriol about her other parent during a car ride to her tournament. A child who then writes "some days I don't want to be here anymore" within days. A child who then gets into a physical altercation at a team trip. A child who then shows up in a video being pulled by the hair.

These are not separate events. They are the same event, unfolding.

Her father says this gently, and only because his daughter needs to hear it from someone: Kassidy, none of that was your fault. A child who is being used as a container for adult rage will find ways, somewhere, to let some of it out. It comes out sideways — in schoolyard fights, in hair-pulling, in dark sentences typed to a friend. The container was never supposed to hold what was being poured into it. You were not built to carry someone else's anger toward your father. No twelve-year-old is.

Your father saw the change in you. He saw it the way a parent sees a weather change across their child's face. And he could not reach into that car and stop what was happening on that drive, any more than he could reach into the apartment in 2019 and pull back the sentence "mom says you ruined our family" before it got to you.

But he documented it. He shared the timeline with your school and your counselors, not to "win," but so that the adults around you in both places could see the whole picture and support you consistently. He went to court and asked for things like a right of first refusal and an authorized caregiver designation for Jesica — small, unglamorous, administrative requests — because the thing he could offer you was structure where the adults in the picture had stopped providing it.

One day, when you are older, you will remember that tournament ride on your own. You will remember how it felt to be twelve and trapped in a car with words that were never supposed to reach you. When that memory surfaces, remember this too: your father saw it. Your father heard about it. Your father believed you. Your father did not make you prove it, did not dismiss it, did not tell you to be quieter about it, did not ask you to choose a side. He simply kept building the place you could always come back to.

A father cannot always intercept the ride. But he can make sure that when it's over, his daughter arrives somewhere safe.

Chapter Sixteen

High Conflict, and the Long Game of Truth

A high-conflict, narcissistic co-parent is not a phrase Jeremiah threw around. It is a clinical reality he lived inside of. The pattern is predictable once you know how to see it: bitterness dressed up as concern, rewriting history out loud, recruiting the children as witnesses and then as weapons, speaking about him in his absence so that his image in his daughters' minds could be edited while he wasn't in the room to defend it.

To his daughters, if you are reading this, here is how you see through it.

Watch for a few things. Watch for who speaks about the other parent when that parent is not there to answer. Watch for who uses you as a messenger. Watch for who needs you to agree that the other one is bad, and who is just happy to see you happy. Watch for who pays, quietly, for years, without making you feel the weight of it. Watch for who shows up — not loudly, but again, and again, and again.

Bitter words are loud but weightless. Facts are quiet but they do not move.

The facts about your father are these:

  • He came from nothing and became someone his colleagues openly admire.
  • He taught himself his entire career.
  • He has paid, to date, over $190,000 to make sure you were provided for — and he will keep paying, because that is what he does.
  • He built and maintained a home for you with him, on his own time, at real cost to himself.
  • He fought — through courts, through exhaustion, through heartbreak — to stay in your lives as a father, not a check.
  • He cried for you on nights you will never know about, because he could not reach you in moments you needed him.
  • He never stopped. Not once.

Whatever you have been told about him in rooms he wasn't in — hold it up against that list. If it does not survive the comparison, it is not the truth. It is noise.

Chapter Seventeen

The Man He Became

The little boy who bounced between four schools before he finished fourth grade grew up to build a career, build a family, and build a life on his own strength — with no inherited map and no safety net.

He was supposed to be a statistic. He became a mentor. He was supposed to be absent. He became the most present father he was legally, physically, and financially permitted to be — and then some. He was supposed to break. He paid $190,000 and counting, and he is still standing.

Resilience is not a word he would use about himself. He is too busy living it to narrate it. But anyone who has watched his life — really watched it — knows exactly what it looks like.

It looks like him.

Epilogue

To the Three Girls This Was Really Written For

Your father is not a perfect man. No honest biography pretends otherwise. But he is a real one — the kind who stays, who pays, who fights, who builds, who cries alone so that you don't have to, and who refuses, absolutely refuses, to become the absent father he himself grew up without.

One day, when the noise quiets and you are old enough to look at the full picture with your own eyes, you will see what the people around him have always seen.

You will see a self-made man who was handed nothing.

You will see a father who was given every reason to walk away and who chose, every single day, to stay.

You will see him.

And you will know.
